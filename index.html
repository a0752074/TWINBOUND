FLIGHT NOTES

Ready for departure.

01

Design and plan

Choose a destination and load a reference transfer. Configure a science objective and check its instrument, launch mass, and budget. Drag the arrow or change the date to compare trajectories.

02

Thrust, then coast

Left, right, and up (A, D, W) use fuel. Down, S, or releasing the keys stops thrust while momentum remains. Counter-thrust can reduce sideways velocity.

03

Collect and navigate

Orange crystals add fuel, green crystals add energy, and repair pickups restore hull cells. Skim a comet's glow and avoid its core. Use short burns and correct course early. A large departure from the route can exceed your remaining correction fuel.

04

Deliver the mission

Science runs automatically near the destination. Instruments use power and storage; the radio sends data to Earth with light-time delay. Reach the destination, then review the flight score and science requirements separately.

Optional 3D cockpit: press V during flight. W/S controls forward/reverse thrust, A/D moves left/right, and Q/E descends/ascends. Space coasts. Only points earned while 3D is active receive the extra 25%; switching views gives no points. The contact radar shows the same obstacles and supplies as 2D; edge arrows mark objects above or below the window.

Physics, operations, and sources

Moon, Mars, and Pluto: sampled NASA/JPL Horizons positions and velocities drive the 11-body Newtonian gravity model. A Dormand–Prince 5(4) adaptive integrator propagates the spacecraft. The live flight starts from the predicted initial state. Pluto and outer planets use system barycenters.

Launch epoch: dates mean 00:00 TDB, not local midnight. Launches are supported from 2025-01-01 through 2030-12-31, with trajectory data through 2051-01-01. No extrapolation. Flight starts at an external injection-service handoff, not at ground liftoff.

Controls and time: the 2D controls produce 3D delta-v using rocket-equation fuel accounting. Active burns use control seconds; the remaining interval is compressed coast time. Displayed relative speed comes from the 3D velocity difference. The local dodge scene uses a separate display scale.

Route stability: position, velocity, future encounter, and remaining correction fuel determine a smoothed risk indicator. Corrections can restore it. Limited navigation assistance also consumes fuel. Arrival means a flyby within a distance tolerance, not orbit insertion or landing.

Science design: objectives require a compatible instrument. Payload and radio mass change acceleration and delta-v; their power load drains the existing battery. Full storage drops observations. The link rate uses inverse-square distance and a cap; solar occultation blocks the link. Distance and light time use the live JPL-based state.

Operations time: observation and data rates use control seconds, like the original battery economy. Signal delay uses physical TDB days. This mixed-time model is educational; it is not an end-to-end engineering link budget. Equipment, prices, capacities, and rates are game estimates. Ground access is continuous except solar occultation.

Proxima Centauri: an ideal external drive accelerates at 1g up to 0.8c and later decelerates. Earth and onboard clocks use special relativity. The local dodge state is separate from the relativistic drive. Science completion uses an onboard archive; Earth delivery is reported separately. This route does not use a measured Proxima trajectory.

Fictional encounters: random rocks, rapid resource collection, repairs, and black holes are game mechanics. Fixed power generation preserves the original game economy and does not model sunlight or years of energy autonomy. Archived NASA and partner images are collection rewards, not new photographs taken by the player.

Progress follows the physical trajectory. Pause and inactive tabs stop the game. A depleted battery or destroyed hull ends the flight. Empty fuel still allows coasting.
